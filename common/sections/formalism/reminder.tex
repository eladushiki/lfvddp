In our original work~\cite{LastDDPPaper}, we postulated the signal and null hypotheses as:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:last_ddp_paper_hypotheses}
\begin{aligned}
    \nullhypothesis &: \quad
    \begin{cases}
        \pdf_{\dataset{A}}(\eventsymbol)
        &=
        \frac{N_{\dataset{A}}}
            {\int \refdist[\eventsymbol]\,d\eventsymbol}
        e^{h(\eventsymbol)} \refdist[\eventsymbol],
        \\
        \pdf_{\dataset{B}}(\eventsymbol)
        &=
        \frac{N_{\dataset{B}}}
            {\int \refdist[\eventsymbol]\,d\eventsymbol}
        e^{h(\eventsymbol)+r} \refdist[\eventsymbol],
    \end{cases} \\
    \hypothesis{1} &: \quad
    \begin{cases}
        \pdf_{\dataset{A}}(\eventsymbol)
        &=
        \frac{N_{\dataset{A}}}
            {\int \refdist[\eventsymbol]\,d\eventsymbol}
        e^{f(\eventsymbol)} \refdist[\eventsymbol],
        \\
        \pdf_{\dataset{B}}(\eventsymbol)
        &=
        \frac{N_{\dataset{B}}}
            {\int \refdist[\eventsymbol]\,d\eventsymbol}
        e^{g(\eventsymbol)} \refdist[\eventsymbol].
    \end{cases}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent Here, $\hypothesis{1}$ and $\nullhypothesis$ are the signal and null hypotheses, accordingly. The $\pdf$'s are probability distributions, that we assume to be the underlying distributions of either of the samples - $\dataset{A}$ and $\dataset{B}$. All distributions reference a common probability distribution, $\refdist[\eventsymbol]$, that is taken to be the combined sample, and allows us to easily compare between them, $\eventsymbol$ being a single real number that fully describes an event. $f$ and $g$ express shifts between the two distributions due to a signal, while $h$ and $r$ express a communal shift due to system effects and scale effects only. The rest are normalization factors.

$f, g, h$ and $r$ are parameters of the hypotheses, hence this formulates a hypothesis space. Given two samples, neural networks are used to find the best fitting hypothesis parameters from within each space. As claimed in \cite{LastDDPPaper}, $h$ and $r$ are the most accurate at an analytic 0. $h$ for the distributions are of the same shape, $r$ for the scale factor is swallowed in the normalization factor in any case. So we won't further bother to write them in the first place.
