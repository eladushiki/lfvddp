In previous work~\cite{Italian_no_1_Learning_New_Physics_from_a_Machine, Italian_no_2_Learning_New_Physics_from_an_Imperfect_Machine}, the signal hypothesis is written as a multiplicative deformation of $\refdist$, parametrized by a fully connected neural network with N hidden neurons, similarly to Eq.~\ref{eq:last_ddp_paper_hypotheses}. The resulting test statistic (Eq.~\ref{eq:inverse_log_likelihood_ratio}) did not converge accurately to the $\chi^2$ distribution predicted by Wilks' theorem.

Agreement between the empirical distribution and the expected $\chi^2$ curve can reflect parameter tuning rather than physical behavior. The earlier study itself indicates that the picture is more complicated: Section IV D of Ref.~\cite{Italian_no_1_Learning_New_Physics_from_a_Machine} discusses this issue, and Section IV C presents discrepancies in two-dimensional examples.

Our previous study~\cite{LastDDPPaper} encountered the same difficulty. The symmetrized method removes the need for weight clipping, but agreement with the theoretical distribution still requires fixed hyperparameters. The discussion of overfitting in Section 6.2 of that work makes this dependence explicit.

Two measures were introduced to address this behavior:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A cutoff is imposed on the \gls{nn} weights and biases (weight clipping).
    \item The training hyperparameters, including the number of epochs and learning rate, are fixed.
\end{itemize}

Examining the applicability of the Wilks and Wald theorems to this method suggests a possible explanation.

\subsection{Broken Wilks and Wald}

Recall the output of the \gls{nn}:

\begin{equation}
    f(\anyevent | v_i, w_i, b_i) = b_0 + \sum\limits_{j=1}^4 v_j \sigma(w_j \anyevent + b_j)
\end{equation}

The null hypothesis is represented in the signal hypothesis space by $f = 0$. One such parametrization is $b_0 = v_i = 0$, with arbitrary values of the other parameters. At this point, $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial w_j} = 0 \forall j$ and $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial b_i} = 0 \forall i \neq 0$. Entire rows and columns of the Fisher information therefore vanish, and its rank is at most 5.

The null set is even larger considering parameter combinations such as $w_j=0$ for all $j$ with $b_0=-\sum_jv_j\sigma(b_j)$, and pairs of identical hidden units with $v_j=-v_{j'}$. Hidden-unit permutation symmetry makes the Fisher matrix singular even away from the null.

For the asymptotic $\chi^2$ degrees of freedom to equal the free parameter count, the requirements include~\cite{Wilks_Original, Wald_Original}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The null hypothesis should be identified with one set of parameters within the signal hypothesis space. That is, a "$\param$ = 0" as in signal strength hypothesis testing.
    \item The Fisher information matrix of the signal hypothesis space is nonsingular.
\end{enumerate}

These conditions are required for the limit to hold. The observed behavior is therefore only an approximation to the expected distribution.

The hidden-layer parameters are identified only under $H_1$; this is the Davies problem. The null distribution of $q_0$ is that of the supremum of a $\chi^2$ random field. It is stochastically larger than $\chi^2_{N+1}$, and in general it is not $\chi^2_{(k+2)N+1}$.

\subsubsection{Possible solutions}
To obtain the exact distribution and evaluate the sensitivity without modeling the background \gls{pdf}, several approaches can be considered:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Parametrize a \gls{nn}'s output in a way such that the null hypothesis is expressible by only a single set of parameter values.
    \item Parametrize $f$ in some other way, such that the function space is spanned in a clear way by a well-defined number of basis elements.
    \item Tune the hyperparameters to achieve the expected distribution and keep them fixed, as was done until now. \label{item:param_fix}
    \item In addition to \ref{item:param_fix}, generate the expected background only distribution by shuffling and compare solely to it, rather than expecting any theoretical behavior, as was done in \cite{LastDDPPaper}.
\end{enumerate}

The last two options were discussed in previous work and are used extensively here. We also implemented the other options to study their behavior. Their performance appears to vary across the configurations considered above.
