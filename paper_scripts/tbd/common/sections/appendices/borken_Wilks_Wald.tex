\label{sec:broken_Wilks_Wald}

In previous work \cite{Italian_no_1_Learning_New_Physics_from_a_Machine, Italian_no_2_Learning_New_Physics_from_an_Imperfect_Machine} the signal hypothesis (similarly to Eq. \ref{eq:last_ddp_paper_hypotheses}) is expressed as a multiplicative transformation of the $\refdist$ and an output of a N-neuron hidden layer fully connected neural network.
A problem arose from that - the convergence of the test statistic (Eq. \ref{eq:inverse_log_likelihood_ratio}) to the Wilks theorem's predicted $\chi^2$ was not accurate.

While this assimilates the evident distributions to the expected $\chi^2$ contour lines, one could claim that this is not a physical phenomenon, but merely engineered parameters to comply with the theory. In the paper itself there is evidence that this is not the whole picture - section IV D in \cite{Italian_no_1_Learning_New_Physics_from_a_Machine} discusses it, and discrepancies in a 2D examples are presented in section IV C.

Our group's previous work \cite{LastDDPPaper} experienced this problem as well. While figuring out a way to bypass the weight clipping with the symmetrized mechanism, a fixation of the hyper parameters is still needed to get theory-compliant results. In section 6.2 there, overfitting is discussed. It's clear that the procedure is indeed dependent on hyper parameters.

Two coping mechanisms were introduced:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A cutoff was set for the \gls{nn}'s weights and biases ("weight clipping")
    \item The model training's hyper parameters (number of epochs, learning rate, etc.) are fixed
\end{itemize}

Our investigation of the Wilks and Wald's theorems' validity with regards to the methods offers a possible explanation.

\subsection{Broken Wilks and Wald}
 
Let's reiterate our \gls{nn}'s output formula:

\begin{equation}
    f(\anyevent | v_i, w_i, b_i) = b_0 + \sum\limits_{j=1}^4 v_j \sigma(w_j \anyevent + b_j)
\end{equation}

In our formulation, the null hypothesis is equivalent to the point in the signal hypothesis space in which $f = 0$. It is described by the parameterization: $b_0 = v_i = 0$, while the other parameters can carry any value. In that point also $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial w_j} = 0 \forall j$ and $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial b_i} = 0 \forall i \neq 0$. These terms nullify entire columns and rows of the Fisher information, which is now of the rank of at most 5.

The null set is even larger considering parameter combination such as $w_j=0$ for all $j$ with $b_0=-\sum_jv_j\sigma(b_j)$, and pairs of identical hidden units with $v_j=-v_{j'}$. Hidden-unit permutation symmetry makes the Fisher matrix singular even away from the null.

For the free parameter count to be the degree of the converged $\chi^2$, the following (among other stuff) is required \cite{Wilks_Original, Wald_Original}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The null hypothesis should be identified with one set of parameters withing the signal hypothesis space. That is, a "$\param$ = 0" as in signal strength hypothesis testing.
    \item The Fisher information matrix of the signal hypothesis space is non singular
\end{enumerate}

For the limit to hold, these should too. Hence, what we evidently saw, was merely an approximation of the behavior we knew to be looking for.

The hidden-layer parameters are identified only under $H_1$; this is the Davies problem. The null distribution of $q_0$ is that of the supremum of a $\chi^2$ random field. It is stochastically larger than $\chi^2_{N+1}$, and in general it is not $\chi^2_{(k+2)N+1}$.

\subsubsection{Possible solutions}
To solve this and get the exact distribution, in order to estimate the predicting power of our method without modeling the background \gls{pdf}, a few directions might be chosen:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Parametrize a \gls{nn}'s output in a way such that the null hypothesis is expressible by only a single set of parameter values.
    \item Parametrize $f$ in some other way, such that the function space is spanned in a clear way by a well understood number of base elements.
    \item Engineer the hyper parameters to achieve the expected distribution and keep them fixed, as was done until now. \label{item:param_fix}
    \item In addition to \ref{item:param_fix}, generate the expected background only distribution with a shuffling practice and compare solely to it, rather than expecting any theoretical behavior, as was done in \cite{LastDDPPaper}.
\end{enumerate}

While the last two options are discussed in previous work and are widely used in our paper as well, we implemented all options to experiment with them. The general feeling is that they work variably well in the different configurations shown above.

\subsubsection{Example: convergence in 2D}

Without any restriction for \gls{nn}'s weights and biases, in the form of weight clipping or Adam optimizer weight decay, we experience a virtually-unbounded improvement of the loss minimization in our case. While seeing reasonably convincing convergence after a few tens of thousands of epochs, as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:2d-nonconverging-percentiles}, we can go on and see improvement of the resulting $t$ as long as we keep training.
