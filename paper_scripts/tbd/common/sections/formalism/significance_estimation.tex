\subsubsection{Effect of Existing Signal}

What happens in the optimization process is that the flexibility of the signal hypothesis' signal definition captures the most-likely accidental signal-like deviation that stems from the Poisson statistics that govern the problem, and does not originate from a real signal. This is the probability distribution that is captured by Wilks and Wald, and is closely related to the look elsewhere effect.

Any signal that exists in either of our samples, that is captured by our definition of what is a signal, should favor the signal hypothesis in the LR test (Eq.~\ref{eq:inverse_log_likelihood_ratio}) and generate larger $t$ values than those of the background only case. Our test for rejecting the background only hypothesis is then quantified by a significance measure,

\begin{equation}
    Z = \Phi^{-1}(1-p)
\end{equation}

\noindent $\Phi$ being the inverse of the cumulative distribution of the Gaussian, $p$ is the p-value to get an equally or greater incompatibility with the background only hypothesis from a true background only distribution.

\subsubsection{Significance Estimation}

The significance estimation of a $\invllratio$ value is carried out in several different ways;


    \paragraph{Theoretic significance from PDFs.}

    If analytic expressions of the PDFs of the distributions are known, we can express

    \begin{wideequations}
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:analytic_significance_from_pdfs}
        Z_\text{analytic} = \sqrt{\invllratio} = \sqrt  {
            2 \left( -N_s + \int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{k}} d\anyevent (N_b(\anyevent) + N_s(\anyevent)) \log \dfrac{N_s(\anyevent) + N_b(\anyevent)}{N_b(\anyevent)} \right)
        }
    \end{equation}
\end{wideequations}

    with $N_s$ the \gls{ndf} of signal events at a point $\event$ and $N_b$ the number of background events.

    \paragraph{Binned evident significance from run parameters.}

    If the \glspl{ndf} are not necessarily known, but the mean amount of events is chosen when generating the datasets, we can rewrite

    \begin{wideequations}
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:binwise_significacne}
        Z_\text{evident} = \sqrt{\invllratio} = \sqrt{
            2 \left( -N_s + \sum\limits_{\event} (N_b(\event) + N_s(\event)) \log \dfrac{N_s(\event) + N_b(\event)}{N_b(\event)} \right)
        }
    \end{equation}
\end{wideequations}

\paragraph{Black box relative to $\chi^2$ or shuffle distribution.}

Either directly from the theoretical distribution of the test statistic or by direct estimation from running many experiments shuffling the two sets of samples, we have a solid estimation of the distribution of $\invllratio$. By comparing a value to it, we can estimate the significance with which we can claim for a discovery from a single $\invllratio$. In practice, we repeat the process and take the significance of the mean.
