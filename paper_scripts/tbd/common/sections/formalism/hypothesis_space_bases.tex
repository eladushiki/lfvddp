\subsubsection{Expressing the Hypothesis Spaces}

Previously~\cite{LastDDPPaper}, we used a neural network to express the signal hypothesis space. The arguments for choosing it are straight forward - it is a highly expressive function space, that can fit various signal shapes and strengths, while having a clear and sizable set of parameters, that is its degrees of freedom (which will matter in a bit). The downsize is an overfitting tendency and is only approximately conforming of Wilks and Wald (Section~\ref{sec:Wilks_Wald}), a problem which was briefly discussed in \cite{LastDDPPaper} and is further explained in \iftoggle{ispaper}{Appendix}{Section}~\ref{sec:broken_Wilks_Wald}.

Our 3 layered, fully connected, sigmoid activated 
\gls{nn}'s output is expressed by:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:neural_net_explicit}
    f(\event) = b_{0} + \sum\limits_{\alpha = 1}^{N} \omega_{\alpha} \sigma(\sum\limits_{j = 1}^{k}\omega^j_{\alpha} \event^j + b_\alpha).
\end{equation}

The \gls{dof} of a hypothesis space spanned by a \gls{nn}, then, goes by

\begin{equation}
    N_\text{DOF} = (k + 2) \times N + 1
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{$\chi^2$}{Chi-squared} Degrees of Freedom (DOF)}
When considering the free parameters of either hypothesis space the following calculation follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item In $\hypothesis{1}$, the degrees of freedom are in $f$ and $\nparam$ and $\refdist$.
    \item In $\nullhypothesis$, the degrees of freedom are in $\nparam$ and $\refdist$.
\end{itemize}

When counting the \gls{dof} of a hypothesis, we need to take into consideration those of $f$ and those of $\nparam$. This being said, the behavior of the $t$ distribution depends on the difference between the number of \gls{dof} of the singnal and null spaces. The null hypothesis sapce containing the $\nparam$ \gls{dof} as well, we get that the difference is exactly the \gls{dof} of $f$.

Wilks' theorem requires an identifiable model, with the null as a single

fail for the network parametrization (note~19). Eq.~(24) should

distribution. (The null result is Wilks [11]; Wald [12] concerns the
behaviour under alternatives.)\par}\medskip

\iftrue % MATH NOTE 11  (to hide: change \iftrue to \iffalse)
\par\medskip\noindent{\color{blue}\raggedright\textbf{[Math note 11 -- permutation calibration with an active nuisance]}\enspace
A permutation test is valid only if A and B are exchangeable under the
null, i.e.\ $\theta\equiv0$. The null of interest has $\theta\neq0$,
$\theta_{\rm SR}=\theta_{\rm CR}$ and no signal. Permuting then erases
$\theta$ and tests the stronger null $p_A=p_B$. The resulting
distribution misses three contributions: the error in $\theta$
transferred from the CR to the SR, the capacity limits of the $\theta$
network, and the misspecification of note~3. It is therefore too narrow,
and significances are overstated.
 
Null-preserving alternative (a parametric bootstrap, also called a
conditional randomization test):
\begin{enumerate}
\item Fit $H_0$ to get $\hat\pi_0(\vec x,R)$.
\item For $m=1,\dots,M$: keep all $\vec x_i$ and regions, draw
  $y_i\sim{\rm Bernoulli}\big(\hat\pi_0(\vec x_i,R_i)\big)$, refit both
  hypotheses, and record $q_0^{(m)}$.
\item Report $p=\big(1+\#\{q_0^{(m)}\ge q_0^{\rm obs}\}\big)/(M+1)$.
\end{enumerate}
Validate it by checking that the unmodified $\tau\mu/\tau e$ simulation
gives uniform $p$-values.\par}\medskip
\fi % end of MATH NOTE 11
