\paragraph{Expressing the Hypothesis Spaces}

Following Ref.~\cite{LastDDPPaper}, we use a neural network to parametrize the signal hypothesis space. Neural networks can represent a wide range of signal shapes and strengths with an explicit, adjustable number of parameters, which sets the nominal number of degrees of freedom. However, they are susceptible to overfitting, and the Wilks--Wald behavior is only approximate (Section~\ref{sec:Wilks_Wald}). This limitation was discussed briefly in Ref.~\cite{LastDDPPaper} and is examined further in \iftoggle{ispaper}{Appendix}{Section}~\ref{sec:broken_Wilks_Wald}.

The output of our three-layer, fully connected \gls{nn} with sigmoid activation is

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:neural_net_explicit}
    f(\event) = b_{0} + \sum\limits_{\alpha = 1}^{N} \omega_{\alpha} \sigma(\sum\limits_{j = 1}^{k}\omega^j_{\alpha} \event^j + b_\alpha).
\end{equation}

The output is passed through tanh to prevent divergences of the logarithms in the likelihood.

The number of \gls{dof} of the hypothesis space spanned by the \gls{nn} is then

\begin{equation}
    N_\text{DOF} = (k + 2) \times N + 1
\end{equation}

\paragraph{\texorpdfstring{$\chi^2$}{Chi-squared} Degrees of Freedom (DOF)}
The free parameters enter the two hypothesis spaces as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item In $\hypothesis{1}$, the degrees of freedom are in $f$ and $\nparam$ and $\refdist$.
    \item In $\nullhypothesis$, the degrees of freedom are in $\nparam$ and $\refdist$.
\end{itemize}

The parameter count includes the \gls{dof} of both $f$ and $\nparam$. The distribution of $t$, however, depends on the difference between the numbers of \gls{dof} in the signal and null spaces. Since $\nparam$ also enters the null hypothesis, this difference is the number of \gls{dof} of $f$.
