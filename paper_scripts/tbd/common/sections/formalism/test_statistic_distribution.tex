\paragraph{Background Only Distribution}
\label{sec:Wilks_Wald}

The theorems of Wilks~\cite{Wilks_Original} and Wald~\cite{Wald_Original} predict the asymptotic behavior of the test statistic. According to these theorems, the distribution of $\invllratio$ should approach a $\chi^2_d$ distribution,

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:inverse-log-likelihood-ratio-limit}
    \invllratio \sim \chi^2_{d}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $d$ is the difference between the numbers of degrees of freedom in the signal and null hypotheses.

Previous studies~\cite{LastDDPPaper, Italian_no_2_Learning_New_Physics_from_an_Imperfect_Machine}, as well as the reproduction presented below, show approximate agreement with this behavior. It is not, however, an exact limit for this parametrization. The theorems of Wilks~\cite{Wilks_Original} and Wald~\cite{Wald_Original} require an identifiable model, a null hypothesis represented by a single point, and nonsingular Fisher information. The \gls{nn} parametrization does not satisfy these conditions: in Eq.~\ref{eq:neural_net_explicit}, vanishing $w_\alpha$ weights describe the null hypothesis for arbitrary values of the remaining parameters. The expected statistical behavior is therefore a heuristic approximation.

In our experiments, spanning the function space with a linear combination of basis elements gives Eq.~\ref{eq:inverse-log-likelihood-ratio-limit} as an exact limit. This choice introduces other difficulties, which are not discussed here.

Thus, in the absence of a new signal in the \gls{sr}, the number of free parameters in the signal hypothesis can be used to approximate the distribution of $\invllratio$.
